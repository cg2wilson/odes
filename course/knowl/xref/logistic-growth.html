Example 1.2.6 Logistic Growth/Decay.

Exponential growth is not realistic for most population situations. Environmental constraints such as access to food, shelter, etc. will naturally limit the rate of population growth.
🔗
Let’s make the assumption that the rate of change of population is positive until a certain value is reached; call that number \(N\text{.}\) After the population reaches \(N\text{,}\) the growth rate becomes negative (note: not the population, its growth rate). Based on these assumptions, we modify the exponential growth equation by multiplying by a function \(f(P)\text{:}\)
\begin{equation*} \dfrac{dP}{dt} = kP\cdot f(P) \end{equation*}
What does \(f(P)\) look like? When \(P\) is smaller than \(N\text{,}\) we want \(f(P)\) to be close to 1; when \(P \gt N\text{,}\) we want \(f(P)\lt 0\text{.}\) After some experimentation, we might settle on \(f(P) = 1 - \dfrac{P}{N}\text{.}\) In particular, this tells us that when \(P = N\text{,}\) there is zero population growth, which makes sense based on our assumptions.
🔗
Now we have
\begin{equation*} \dfrac{dP}{dt} = kP\lrpar{1-\dfrac{P}{N}} \end{equation*}
which is a separable equation. You should check (this requires partial fraction decomposition, some log rules, and algebra) that the solution is given by
\begin{equation*} P = \dfrac{N}{1+Ce^{-kt}} \end{equation*}
here \(N\) is called the carrying capacity of the population/system.
